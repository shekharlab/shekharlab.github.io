<p class="lab-philosophy">We are an interdisciplinary group working at the interface of molecular neuroscience, computational biology, and biophysics.</p>

<p class="lab-preamble">Many of our questions span multiple length and timescales, from ions moving around a single channel to whole tissues, and from microseconds to hundreds of millions of years of evolution. Chemical engineering, a discipline built on connecting molecular processes to the behavior of whole systems, is a remarkably natural home for this work. Although our group is small, it is intellectually diverse, bringing together chemical engineers, physicists, neuroscientists, and computational biologists. Our primary aim is to advance basic understanding, but we also want our work to be useful to others pursuing complementary questions. The group therefore has a highly collaborative ethos. Our research is organized around three areas.</p>

<section class="research-area">
<h2>Neurogenomics of development and evolution</h2>

<p>The vertebrate brain is built from thousands of neuron types, each with its own molecular identity, shape, and wiring. How does a neuron settle on what it will become? And how has evolution remodeled this cast of cells to suit each species' way of life? We read these histories directly from molecular data, combining single-cell and spatial genomics with statistical inference. Our main model is the retina, the most accessible part of the brain, along with the visual cortex.</p>

<p>We find that neuronal identity is often settled gradually, through an interplay of genetic programs and sensory experience whose balance differs from one cell type to the next (<a href="https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2421022122">Xie et al., <i>PNAS</i> 2025</a>; <a href="https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0165-25.2025">Somaiya et al., <i>J. Neurosci.</i> 2025</a>; <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.09.22.677893v1">Yoo et al., <i>bioRxiv</i> 2025</a>). Across vertebrates, many retinal neuron types turn out to be remarkably ancient. Evolution has reshaped vision largely by tuning the abundance, placement, and gene expression of cell types that date back to early vertebrates (<a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06638-9">Hahn et al., <i>Nature</i> 2023</a>; <a href="https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(25)00376-8">Tommasini et al., <i>Curr. Biol.</i> 2025</a>).</p>

<section class="research-area">
<h2>Spatial biology of degeneration and aging</h2>

<p>In neurodegenerative disease, neurons do not die at random. In glaucoma, the leading cause of irreversible blindness, some retinal ganglion cell types are lost early while others endure. We think part of the answer lies in where a neuron lives, and we build spatial genomic approaches to map cell types across intact tissue and follow their fates in disease.</p>

<p>We are now bringing this spatial view to aging. With Zhigang He's lab, we are asking how the optic nerve changes with age and what limits its ability to regenerate after injury. As part of the Glaucoma Research Foundation's <a href="https://glaucoma.org/research/catalyst-for-a-cure">Catalyst for a Cure</a> (CFC4) team, we search for mechanisms that glaucoma shares with other neurodegenerative diseases, including the immune and glial responses that shape neuronal survival (<a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41590-023-01437-w">Benhar et al., <i>Nat. Immunol.</i> 2023</a>; <a href="https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2026.02.030">Delivanoglou et al., <i>Neuron</i> 2026</a>).</p>

<section class="research-area">
<h2>Microscopic bioelectricity</h2>

<p>Every thought, heartbeat, and sensation begins with ions flowing through channels in a membrane only a few nanometers thick. Yet the textbook picture of neuronal electricity, inherited from Hodgkin and Huxley in the 1950s, treats the membrane as a capacitor and channels as resistors, averaging away the physics around each channel. We start with a simple question: what happens, physically, when a single ion channel opens?</p>

<p>Our theory shows that current through a channel creates a spatially structured, evolving voltage disturbance whose reach is set by the membrane's dielectric properties, geometry, and confinement. It also clarifies when the classical circuit picture emerges (<a href="https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.111.064412">Farhadi et al., <i>Phys. Rev. E</i> 2025</a>; <a href="https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/abstract/10.1103/m446-4lnj">Fernandes et al., <i>Phys. Rev. Research</i> 2026</a>). Electrical and mechanical forces are intertwined: membrane thickness and surface charge govern how electric fields destabilize a bilayer (<a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23477">Ning et al., <i>arXiv</i> 2026</a>).</p>

<p>If neighboring channels feel each other through these fields, the membrane becomes a many-body system. Just as the many forms of ice cannot be deduced from a single water molecule, channel ensembles may support collective bioelectric phases that single-channel models miss. Supported by the <a href="https://chemistry.berkeley.edu/news/philomathia-prize-advances-novel-work-physics-bioelectricity">Philomathia Prize</a>, we are developing a theory of these bioelectric phase transitions and asking how they shape excitability and disease.</p>

  <p><strong>Spotlight.</strong> What happens when ions cross a membrane at a single point? Led by postdoc Hyeongjoo Row and graduate student Joshua Fernandes, we used theory and simulation to follow the resulting electric field in space and time. Localized transport generates long-ranged fields with three regimes: a constant-potential near field, an intermediate monopolar region, and a far-field dipolar region. Under sustained transport, the monopolar region expands at roughly 40 m/s in physiological settings, far faster than ions can diffuse (<a href="https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.7.013185">Row, Fernandes et al., <i>Phys. Rev. Research</i> 2025</a>).</p>
</div>
